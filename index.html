What I've built

Talent intelligence

Lighthouse

A dashboard I designed and built around my own job search, tuned to the exact titles, markets and pay I'm after, for permanent and contract roles alike. The market is competitive, and by the time a role is advertised everyone else has seen it too. So Lighthouse spots companies under hiring pressure before they advertise a role, finds permanent and contract roles that fit me, and drafts the outreach. Nothing gets sent until I've read it and approved it.

How it works

Why I built it

The best technical recruiting happens before the requisition exists. I used to do that by hand: reading funding news, watching careers pages, spotting who was about to have more roles than recruiters. Lighthouse turns that routine into a data pipeline, so my time goes on judgement and conversations.

Ingestion

One adapter per source, all emitting the same typed schema, so a new source is one file and nothing else changes. Signals come from SEC Form D filings, Companies House share-allotment records, funding-news feeds and the public job-board APIs of the major applicant tracking systems (Greenhouse, Lever, Ashby, SmartRecruiters and others). Permanent and contract roles come from six job-board and alert sources. Every request has timeouts, retries and backoff, and any source can fail without taking the rest down.

Data model

Records are resolved to a single company entity, cached in Redis under versioned keys, and snapshotted daily. Two readings of a careers page tell you size; a run of snapshots tells you direction, so Lighthouse tracks hiring velocity, not just headcount. Every action is written to an event log.

Scoring

Pure, unit-tested functions weight four signals: capacity gap (open technical roles per in-house recruiter, 45%), hiring acceleration (25%), funding recency and size (20%) and geographic spread (10%). Small teams are suppressed so the feed isn't noise, and every score ships with its reasons and its caveats. A second model scores each role, permanent or contract, against an evidence bank of my own experience, using a skills taxonomy, and shows the gaps as well as the matches.

Automation and integrations

A tool-calling assistant sits across the data and can query leads and roles, draft outreach and log rejections. It cannot send. Drafts go through a human approval step, then out through the Gmail API with OAuth, and replies are matched back to the thread. Scheduled jobs keep the caches warm, and voice input and output run through speech APIs.

Measuring progress

The Data view reports on the pipeline itself: how fresh each source is, rate-limit use per host, response rate, the email funnel from draft to reply, and why leads were rejected. That's how I tune the scoring, based on what's actually happening in my search.

The repository is private because it holds real search data. A demo mode runs the whole app on invented data and I'm happy to walk you through it live.

Rabbit Hole

How it works

Why I built it

I wanted curiosity to be the way in: no search box, no syllabus, just "what's that?" and a tap. It's also where I practised taking a product from idea to launch and talking about it in public. The direction is a one-stop place to learn a topic, with articles first, then research papers, then video.

Data and APIs

Each world is generated live from one Wikipedia article: its outgoing links become the orbs, with pictures and summaries fetched from the Wikipedia APIs. Research papers come from OpenAlex's open API and carry authors, citation counts and open-access status. The app installs as a web app. A network-first service worker means updates appear on the very next open, and the app itself still opens with no signal.

Automation

The daily challenge is automated end to end. A nightly GitHub Action runs the real app in a headless browser, builds the next two weeks of routes through the link graph and commits each as a data file, so everyone gets the same challenge. A seeded fallback covers any missing day. Share links carry the start, goal and difficulty, so a friend can play or try to beat your result.

The research notebook

Pin tiles, papers and passages with your notes and the trail that led you there. It formats APA, MLA and Harvard references and exports to Word, PDF or Google Docs. It saves on the device with no sign-up, so there's no personal data to look after. Topics are deep links, and the interface runs in six languages.

How it works

Why I'm building it

I kept hearing friends and neighbours say the same thing: issues get reported and never get fixed. So I looked into where the bottleneck sits, and asked whether a public record that tracks every report, with the community confirming it's still a problem, would put more pressure on local authorities than a private inbox ever could. Most reporting tools end the moment you hit submit. This one carries on, so a problem can't quietly disappear.

Data model

A relational Postgres schema covers authorities, wards, postcodes, infrastructure assets, issues, photos, confirmations, comments, followers, notifications and moderation actions. Every status change is written to a history table, so an issue's whole life is auditable. Row-level security controls who can see and change what.

Geographic routing and open data

Locations are stored as PostGIS geography points with spatial indexes, and a database function resolves each report to the right authority. I'm currently replacing the starter boundaries with real local-authority boundary data. Importers load open datasets, such as NaPTAN bus stops and green-space data, as assets a report can attach to. Geography is generic by design, so adding a second council is configuration, not a rebuild.

Community verification

Residents confirm an issue is still a problem, which keeps stale reports honest and surfaces what matters most. Residents appear as a first name and postcode district only. It's independent: not a council site, and it publishes no council response unless a verified authority provides it.

Personal project · ordering in build

VOLT

A training and nutrition platform I use for my own workouts and meals. It ingests every logged workout set and its notes, syncs weekly smart-scale readings, and turns them into progress reports and next week's plan. Ordering and price comparison are in build.

Built withClaude artifactJavaScriptClaude AI coachSmart-scale sync

How it works

Why I built it

I wanted training, food and the weekly shop in one place, with a plan that adjusts to what I actually do. It's the same idea as my recruitment work: capture the data, spot the pattern, act on it.

Data in, and the learning loop

Three streams feed it: sets with weight, reps and free-text notes, daily nutrition and activity against targets, and weekly smart-scale readings of weight, body fat %, muscle mass % and water content. They're stored as time series so it compares trends against the plan. The notes and the trend go to an AI coach that adjusts next week's training.

Nutrition engine

It tracks daily targets for calories, protein, water and steps, balances calories and protein, suggests meals to choose from and writes out the full recipe for each one I pick.

Weekly reporting

Each week the scale data and the training log become a progress report: what changed in weight, body fat, muscle and hydration, how that compares with the plan, and what to do next.

In build: ordering and price comparison

Chosen meals are parsed into ingredients, normalised and merged, then matched to products by barcode and ranked by price per 100 g, with pack sizes optimised. Price adapters feed a cache that shows the age of every price, and never scrape sites that forbid it. It totals the full basket per retailer, delivery included, recommends where to shop, and hands me a suggested order to review and pay for myself. Not live yet.

Planned · intake

Spec Check

Before a search starts, it tests whether the job spec is realistic. It splits a spec into must-haves and nice-to-haves, measures how rare each requirement and combination is across live job postings, and compares the pay with the market. It then shows which requirement narrows the market most, and what dropping it would change.

Planned stackNext.jsTypeScriptClaude APIJob-board feeds

How it would work

Why

Searches are often won or lost at intake. A spec that asks for a market that barely exists costs weeks. I want a hiring manager to see that on day one, with evidence.

What it would do

  • Extract. An LLM turns the spec into structured requirements and separates must-haves from nice-to-haves.
  • Measure rarity. It counts how often each requirement, and each combination, appears in live postings from the public job-board feeds I already ingest for Lighthouse. A rare combination means a narrow market.
  • Check the pay. It compares the offered range with advertised salaries and official earnings data.
  • Test changes. It re-scores the spec with a requirement dropped, to show what would widen the market.
  • Brief. A feasibility score with its reasons and caveats, and a one-page intake brief for the hiring manager conversation.

Honest limits

No open source says how many people hold a given skill, so it estimates rarity from market postings, not headcounts, and says so. It needs no candidate data.

Planned · offer and contract

Offer Desk

Turns an agreed package into an offer letter and a contract draft, using country-specific rules. Starting with three countries, it checks clauses against local rules, compares pay with local bands, and keeps every version and approval in a log. A human signs off. It's a drafting aid, not legal advice.

Planned stackNext.jsTypeScriptClaude APIVersioned templatesEvent log

How it would work

Why

Offers and contracts are where a good process can still go wrong: the wrong notice period, a missing clause, a pay figure that doesn't fit the market. A rules layer can catch these before a human signs off.

What it would do

  • Rule sets. Curated, cited rules per country for notice, probation, holiday, working time and relocation, starting with three countries.
  • Drafting. The agreed package becomes an offer letter and a contract from versioned templates.
  • Checks. Clauses are flagged against the local rules, and the pay is compared with local bands.
  • Control. A human approves, and every version and change is logged.

Honest limits

It's a drafting aid, not legal advice. The rules need a legal or HR check before anyone relies on them, and the demo would use made-up candidates.

Both would plug into a mock applicant tracking system with a shared event log, an approval step and an API, built on made-up data and labelled as such.
