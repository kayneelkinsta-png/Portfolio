Hi. I'm Kayne.
A recruiter who builds.

Twelve years finding engineers, data and security people for some of the hardest-to-hire places around. Lately I've been building the tools I always wished I had.

Why I started building

For most of my career the tools I used belonged to someone else. A search box here, a spreadsheet there, a lot of tabs open at once.

Somewhere along the way I got curious about what sits underneath. How does a funding announcement become a hiring signal? What would it take for a job board, a company registry and my inbox to actually talk to each other? Could sourcing be something I design, not just something I do?

So I started learning by building. AI sourcing, integrations, data modelling and automation are the parts of recruitment I find most interesting right now, and I'd rather understand them properly than watch from the sidelines.

I work with an AI coding assistant as my engineer. I decide what gets built, how it should behave and what it must never do. Then I read it, test it and send it back until it's right.

I'm still learning, and I like it that way. Every project here has made me better at talking to engineers about the roles I'm hiring for.

What I've built

Talent intelligence

Lighthouse

A hub that spots companies under hiring pressure before they advertise a role, finds contract roles for me, and drafts the outreach. Nothing gets sent until I've read it and approved it.

More about Lighthouse

The best technical recruiting happens before the requisition exists. I used to do that by hand: reading funding news, watching careers pages, spotting who was about to have more roles than recruiters. Lighthouse does the legwork so I can spend the time on judgement.

  • Leads scores companies on how many open technical roles each in-house recruiter is carrying, how fast hiring is accelerating, recent funding and geographic spread.
  • Scout finds contract and permanent roles across six sources and scores each against my own experience, showing the gaps as well as the matches.
  • Outreach drafts from a lead's own data. I approve, then it sends from my mailbox and tracks replies.
  • Data reports on its own records: freshness, rate limits, response rates, rejection reasons.

Some decisions I'm proud of: every lead carries the argument against it. Every panel shows how old its data is. It never invents a contact and never guesses a day rate. It doesn't scrape LinkedIn.

The repository is private because it holds real search data. A demo mode runs on invented data and I'm happy to walk you through it live.

Live product

Rabbit Hole

Wikipedia as worlds you can fall into. Every article is a world, every orb is a real article it links to. Dive in, or play the daily challenge and reach a hidden goal in as few worlds as you can.

Try it ↗

More about Rabbit Hole
  • A daily challenge with a streak, and share links that let a friend play or try to beat your result.
  • Research papers appear as a second kind of orb, with authors, citation counts and open-access badges.
  • A notebook where you pin tiles, papers and passages with your notes and the trail that led you there. It formats APA, MLA and Harvard references and exports to Word, PDF or Google Docs. It saves on your device, with no sign-up.
  • Any topic is a shareable link, and the interface works in six languages.

It's where I practised taking a product from idea to launch and talking about it in public.

In build

CivicLens

A map-first platform for reporting local problems with public infrastructure. Residents can confirm an issue is still a problem, so it can't quietly disappear. Starting in Southampton, designed to work for any UK council.

See it. Report it. Confirm it. Track it.

More about CivicLens
  • Consumer-grade and map-first, not a council dashboard.
  • Privacy by design: residents show as a first name and postcode district only.
  • Geography and authority routing are generic, so the second council is configuration, not a rebuild.
  • Independent: not a council site, and it publishes no council response unless a verified authority provides it.

The rules I build by

  • A person sends. The assistant can save a draft. Only I can send it.
  • No invented people. A contact traces to a source or it's labelled a suggestion.
  • Show the counter-argument. Scores come with reasons and caveats, so they can be challenged.
  • Say how old the data is. Every panel carries its freshness.
  • Respect platform terms. No scraping what's off limits; public, documented feeds only.
  • Keep personal data small. CivicLens shows first names only; Rabbit Hole saves on your device.
